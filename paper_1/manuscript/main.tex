\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Regional mineral prospectivity mapping often assumes that predictor--occurrence relationships are constant across a mineralized belt. We examine this assumption in the Central African Copperbelt by applying a Domain-Varying Hierarchical Model (DVHM), a partially pooled Bayesian logistic regression in which intercepts and the linear and quadratic coefficients of fault distance and lithological-contact distance vary by Daly tectonic domain, while Bouguer gravity and surficial-lithology effects are shared. The model is applied to a 1,872-cell nominal $5\,\mathrm{km}\times5\,\mathrm{km}$ grid (138 cells with a compiled occurrence record) and evaluated by holding out each of four contiguous along-belt sectors in turn. The held-out sector is the primary inferential axis; the domain-varying coefficients represent within-fit geological heterogeneity rather than an alternative to spatial validation. The DVHM achieves pooled out-of-fold ROC-AUC of 0.716; a predictor-matched global logistic model with the same four predictors provides a reference at 0.674. Sector-level DVHM ROC-AUC ranges from 0.875 in the south-east to 0.603 in the north-west, and fitted predictor contributions vary along the belt: fault distance dominates in the south-east, whereas the share of Bouguer gravity increases toward the north-west. Across four distance representations, mean ROC-AUC ranges from 0.687 to 0.725. In the two domains with occurrence records, negative associations with contact distance, and with fault distance in NRB\_3a, recur under all four representations, but quadratic curvature and the population-level stationary point $D^*$ are representation-dependent and are not usefully constrained by the data. The results show how domain-varying predictor relationships can be incorporated into regional prospectivity assessment and evaluated under spatial separation; they describe fitted associations and transferability rather than a universal distance relationship or a specific geological mechanism.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} mineral prospectivity mapping; Bayesian hierarchical model; spatial cross-validation; spatial heterogeneity; Central African Copperbelt; Daly tectonic domains; parameter uncertainty

\section{Introduction}

Regional mineral prospectivity mapping integrates geological, geochemical, and geophysical evidence to identify locations associated with known deposits. Published approaches range from knowledge-driven evidence integration \citep{BonhamCarter1994, Carranza2009} to logistic regression, weights of evidence \citep{BonhamCarter1989}, and machine-learning classifiers \citep{Porwal2006, Carranza2015, Ford2019, Yousefi2021, Matthieu2026}. A fundamental question is whether predictor--prospectivity relationships estimated in one part of a mineralized belt remain predictive in geographically separated sectors---and if not, how they vary spatially. In the Central African Copperbelt, regional variation in basin structure, stratigraphy, and ore assemblages \citep{Cailteux2005, Selley2005, Hitzman2012, Key2004, Ndonfack2025} suggests that a single set of global predictor weights may not adequately capture along-belt heterogeneity. Standard random cross-validation can underestimate prediction error when spatially proximate observations share covariates and occurrence histories \citep{Brenning2012, Roberts2017}; the magnitude of this bias depends on the prediction target and blocking design, and spatial blocks can create predictor extrapolation \citep{Valavi2019}.

We address this question using the Domain-Varying Hierarchical Model (DVHM), a partially pooled Bayesian logistic regression in which intercepts and distance-predictor coefficients vary by Daly tectonic domain \citep{Daly2025}, while Bouguer gravity and surficial-lithology effects are shared globally. Spatially varying coefficient structures provide a statistical representation of changing relationships without asserting causation \citep{Gelfand2003, Fotheringham2002}. The Bayesian formulation represents posterior uncertainty in parameters, hierarchical scales, and domain effects, and propagates it to a posterior distribution of the conditional occurrence probability of each held-out cell. Uncertainty in evidence layers, model parameters, conditional probabilities, and outcomes are distinct quantities in prospectivity work \citep{Lisitsin2014, Parsa2021, Zuo2021}. Transfer is evaluated across four geographically contiguous along-belt sectors rather than through random cell splitting. The scientific comparison is therefore among held-out sectors; the domain terms structure each fit rather than define the validation units.

This study applies a partially pooled hierarchical Bayesian prospectivity framework to the Copperbelt and evaluates it under along-belt spatial separation, with a representation-sensitivity design that separates recurring proximity associations from representation-dependent polynomial curvature and from the location of the fitted quadratic stationary point, $D^*$. We ask how out-of-fold discrimination varies among held-out sectors (Section~\ref{sec:res-transfer}); how fault distance, lithological-contact distance, Bouguer gravity, and surficial lithology jointly relate to recorded occurrence labels (Section~\ref{sec:res-coefficients}); how model-component contributions differ across sectors (Section~\ref{sec:res-shares}); and whether distance-response conclusions persist under alternative mathematical representations (Section~\ref{sec:res-sensitivity}). A predictor-matched global model provides a reference for the pooled discrimination of the heterogeneous formulation.

\section{Study area, spatial data, and evidence inventory}

\subsection{Study area and grid definition}
\label{sec:study_area}

The study region encompasses the Central African Copperbelt across the border region of northern Zambia and the southern Democratic Republic of the Congo. The spatial frame is a nominal $5\,\mathrm{km}\times5\,\mathrm{km}$ grid in WGS 84 / UTM Zone 35S (EPSG:32735). A full 8,536-cell grid was generated and intersected with the union of the five USGS permissive-tract polygons, which are distributed in Arc 1950 Lambert Conformal Conic and were reprojected to EPSG:32735. The intersection retained 1,899 cells and clipped edge cells to the tract union; 253 cells retain the full $25\,\mathrm{km}^2$ area. The retained cells cover $21{,}790\,\mathrm{km}^2$ and span approximately $500\,\mathrm{km}$ along strike, with cell centroids from Easting $282{,}675$ to $715{,}465\,\mathrm{m}$ and Northing $8{,}380{,}251$ to $8{,}859{,}512\,\mathrm{m}$. The permissive tracts define the assessment frame; they are not a geological contact or the limit of mineralization. Every retained cell enters the likelihood and all reported metrics with unit weight; clipped cell area is not used as an offset, weight, or normalization factor, so the 253 full-area and 1,646 clipped cells are treated equally.

The observational unit is one nominal $5\,\mathrm{km}\times5\,\mathrm{km}$ grid cell, represented by its clipped polygon, centroid coordinates $(x_i,y_i)$, and a binary compiled-record indicator $Y_i\in\{0,1\}$. The prepared table contains 1,899 cells and 139 positive labels. Twenty-seven cells have no stored domain assignment, including one positive cell; complete-case filtering and removal of unrecognized domain labels therefore yield 1,872 cells, including 138 positive and 1,734 background cells.

The response is cell-level presence, not an occurrence count: a cell is positive when at least one compiled USGS occurrence record falls within its polygon, and repeated records within a cell do not increase its label. The USGS Central African Copperbelt assessment database contains 187 point records: 102 prospects, 65 deposits, and 20 grouped records; grouped records represent multiple sites within 500\,m aggregated for resource modeling. Point-in-polygon assignment places 184 records inside the 1,899-cell frame and three outside; the 184 records map to 139 unique positive cells, of which 107 contain one record and the remainder contain two to five. Background cells indicate that no compiled record falls in the cell, not a verified absence of mineralization. The source metadata report a horizontal positional accuracy of 1,000\,m, source-map scales from 1:5,000 to 1:2,000,000, manual digitization for some sites, and a use constraint that the database not be displayed at scales larger than 1:1,000,000. A 1,000\,m positional uncertainty is one-fifth of the 5\,km cell size, so records near cell edges can be assigned to adjacent cells under a different location interpretation. Accordingly, fitted probabilities describe the conditional probability of a compiled cell label under the model and validation design, not a probability of discovery.

\paragraph{Distance to mapped faults.}
The fault predictor is the Euclidean distance from each cell centroid to the nearest digitized fault line of the structural compilation in Figure~1 of \citet{Selley2005}, georeferenced to EPSG:32735. Neither the positional accuracy of that compilation nor the provenance of the georeferencing control points is documented. The surviving 126-line fault layer reproduces 1,672 of the 1,899 prepared distance values; the remaining 227 differ by up to about 33\,km, so the fault geometry behind the prepared predictor differs from the surviving layer in parts of the frame. The distance is a cartographic proximity measure, not a surveyed distance.

\paragraph{Surficial lithology and contact distance.}
The lithology source is the Africa surficial lithology raster \citep{SayreKabuchanga2015}, a 3 arc-second grid of 19 lithology classes plus water. It compiles and reclassifies twelve digital geology, soil, and lithology databases and mixes bedrock with unconsolidated parent materials; it is therefore not a bedrock host-rock map, and its spatial detail varies across Africa. The raster was reprojected to EPSG:32735 at 93\,m, with nearest-neighbour resampling inferred from the result (Supplementary Section~S1), and clipped to the tract union, within which six classes occur: metasedimentary, silicic, metaigneous, alluvium-fluvial, alluvium-saline, and water. Polygonizing the clipped raster yields 314 polygons and, after removal of the outer tract boundary, 259 internal contact lines.

The contact predictor is the minimum planar distance from each cell centroid to the nearest internal contact line. Because the outer tract boundary is excluded, it measures proximity to internal lithological contacts only. The fitted table contains no zero contact distances: the minimum is 11\,m and the median is 12.0\,km. This differs from a polygon-based contact distance, which assigns zero to a cell touched by a contact.

The surficial-lithology terms in the model use the class field of the prepared cell table, which was inherited from the original feature build. That build was not scripted, so its assignment rule was reconstructed by testing candidate rules against the source raster and contact layer (Supplementary Section~S2). The stored class agrees with the raster class at the cell centroid in only 42\% of cells and with the majority class within the cell in 45\%, but it equals the class attribute of one of the contact lines nearest to the cell polygon in 1,812 of 1,899 cells (95.4\%). Each contact line carries the class of the polygon from which it was traced, and in 1,890 cells the nearest point is shared by contacts of different classes, so the stored class is effectively the class on one side of the nearest contact, chosen without a documented rule. The covariate is therefore a nearest-contact attribute rather than the lithology of the cell, and the surficial-lithology coefficients describe associations with that attribute. Class codes are converted to indicators with metasedimentary as the reference class. In every training partition, four classes (silicic, metaigneous, alluvium-saline, and water) contain both positive and background cells and are retained; alluvium-fluvial contains no positive cell, is never retained, and is therefore pooled with the reference class.

\paragraph{Bouguer gravity.}
The gravity predictor is a 2 arc-minute complete Bouguer anomaly grid (mGal). Its own header names neither the product nor the datum; it is identified as the WGM2012 complete Bouguer anomaly \citep{BGIWGM2012} from the headers of the companion free-air, disturbance, and topography grids, which share its limits and spacing (Supplementary Section~S1). Each cell centroid was transformed to geographic coordinates, assumed to be WGS 84 (EPSG:4326), and assigned the value of the nearest grid node without interpolation; centroid-to-node offsets are 0.07--2.56\,km (median 1.47\,km).

\paragraph{Tectonic domains.}
Domain labels follow the tectonic framework of \citet{Daly2025}, digitized from its Figure~14 as an 11-polygon layer. Cells carry one of six domain labels: Sub-domains~3a and~3b of the inverted Northern Rift Basin (NRB\_3a, the salt-driven fold belt with basement-fault inversion, and NRB\_3b, the Kafue anticline area with major basement-fault inversion and minor salt tectonics); Sub-domain~4b of the high-strain zone, the low-grade part of the Central Rift Zone and later orogen (CRZ\_4b); Domain~1, the Northeastern Katangan Basin (NKB); Domain~2, the Mwashia marginal sub-basins (MMSB); and Domain~5, the inverted Southern Rift Basin (SRB). The stored labels are free-text strings (for example, ``Inverted Salt Province NRB 3a'' and ``LP/LT CRZ 4b''), and the fitting code assigns each to a domain by matching the tokens 3a, 3b, CRZ, SRB, NKB, and MMSB. The 27 cells without a label are excluded (Section~\ref{sec:study_area}). The digitized polygons contain small gaps and overlaps, and the stored labels of 49 complete-case cells (2.8\% of background cells) differ from a strict centroid-in-polygon join. All 49 are background cells; no positive cell in the modelling frame is affected (Supplementary Section~S1).

\subsection{Distribution of positive cells among tectonic domains}
\label{sec:domains}

Cells with a compiled occurrence record are confined to two of the six domains: NRB\_3a contains 107 positive cells among 1,127, and NRB\_3b contains 31 among 550. CRZ\_4b ($n=154$), NKB ($n=24$), MMSB ($n=16$), and SRB ($n=1$) contain no positive cells. The hierarchical model nevertheless estimates parameters for all six domains, linked through the population hyperparameters $\mu_p$ and $\sigma_p$. In the four domains without positive cells the likelihood carries no within-domain positive-class information, so their domain effects are informed by background cells and by partial pooling toward the population distribution; SRB, represented by a single cell, is described almost entirely by that distribution. Domain-specific coefficients in these four domains must therefore be interpreted with caution, and ROC-AUC is undefined within them.

\subsection{Spatial validation design}
\label{sec:validation}

Validation and hierarchical grouping use two distinct spatial stratifications (Figure~\ref{fig:model_workflow}). For validation, cell centroids are projected onto their first principal component, which follows the north-west--south-east trend of the belt, and the ordered cells are divided into four contiguous folds of 468 cells. Fold~1 lies at the south-eastern end of the frame and Fold~4 at the north-western end (Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_validation_design}). Each fold is held out once while the model is fitted to the other three. Contiguous folds reduce local train--test overlap but do not guarantee independence across fold boundaries.

Within each fold, continuous predictors are standardized with training-partition statistics only, and the same transformation is applied to held-out cells; quadratic fault- and contact-distance terms are formed from the standardized values. Lithology classes are retained only when represented by both positive and background cells in the training partition, so held-out data do not influence which classes enter the model.

The six Daly domains \citep{Daly2025} define the hierarchical grouping and are not used to form folds, so this is not leave-one-domain-out validation. NRB\_3a, NRB\_3b, CRZ\_4b, and NKB are represented in every training partition, but SRB lies entirely in Fold~2 and MMSB entirely in Fold~4. When those folds are held out, the corresponding domain is absent from training and its held-out cells receive domain effects drawn from the population distribution rather than effects informed by within-domain data. Domain-level performance metrics are computed post hoc as descriptive strata.

\section{Bayesian hierarchical methodology and spatial validation}

\subsection{Model specification: domain-varying coefficients within each fold}

For cell $i$ in Daly domain $d(i) \in \{1,\dots,6\}$, the DVHM is a partially pooled hierarchical Bayesian logistic regression \citep{GelmanHill2007}:

\begin{equation}
Y_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_i),\qquad \mathrm{logit}(p_i) = \eta_i,
\end{equation}

where the linear predictor $\eta_i$ decomposes as:

\begin{equation}
\eta_i = \alpha_{d(i)} + \beta_{f,\mathrm{lin},d(i)} z_{f,i} + \beta_{f,\mathrm{sq},d(i)} z_{f,i}^2 + \beta_{l,\mathrm{lin},d(i)} z_{l,i} + \beta_{l,\mathrm{sq},d(i)} z_{l,i}^2 + \beta_g z_{g,i} + \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{rock},i}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{rock}}.
\end{equation}

The model distinguishes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Domain-varying parameters:} Domain intercepts $\alpha_d$ and the four distance coefficients (fault linear $\beta_{f,\mathrm{lin},d}$, fault quadratic $\beta_{f,\mathrm{sq},d}$, lithological contact linear $\beta_{l,\mathrm{lin},d}$, and lithological contact quadratic $\beta_{l,\mathrm{sq},d}$).
\item \textbf{Global parameters:} The Bouguer gravity coefficient $\beta_g$ and the surficial-lithology vector $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{rock}}$ are shared globally across all domains.
\end{itemize}

Domain-varying parameters use a non-centred hierarchical parameterization to ensure efficient MCMC exploration:

\begin{equation}
\beta_{p,d} = \mu_p + \sigma_p \cdot \mathrm{offset}_{p,d}, \qquad \text{for } p \in \{f_{\mathrm{lin}}, f_{\mathrm{sq}}, l_{\mathrm{lin}}, l_{\mathrm{sq}}\},
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
\alpha_d = \mu_\alpha + \sigma_\alpha \cdot \mathrm{offset}_{\alpha,d}.
\end{equation}

The model uses the following priors:

\begin{align}
\mu_p &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), & \sigma_p &\sim \mathrm{HalfNormal}(1),\\
\mathrm{offset}_{p,d} &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), & \mu_\alpha &\sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathrm{logit}(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{train}}), 1\right),\\
\sigma_\alpha &\sim \mathrm{HalfNormal}(1), & \mathrm{offset}_{\alpha,d} &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1),\\
\beta_g &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), & \boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{rock}} &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}),
\end{align}

where $\hat{p}_{\mathrm{train}} = \sum y_{\mathrm{train}} / N_{\mathrm{train}}$ anchors the intercept prior to the empirical training base rate. Because $z_{f}$, $z_{l}$, and $z_{g}$ are standardized within each training fold, these priors describe effects per training standard deviation, not effects per kilometre. The model contains no continuously varying spatial random field or spatially correlated residual process; spatial structure enters through the discrete Daly-domain effects, the fitted predictor surfaces, and the held-out spatial design.

The model was fitted in PyMC \citep{Salvatier2016} with the No-U-Turn sampler \citep{Hoffman2014}, using two chains of 2,500 tuning steps and 1,500 retained draws per chain, a target acceptance rate of 0.99, and random seed 42. None of the four fold fits produced divergent transitions; across all parameters of the four fits, the maximum rank-normalized split $\hat{R}$ is 1.004 and the minimum bulk effective sample size is 1,219.

\subsection{Continuous probability prediction and threshold-free evaluation}

For each posterior draw $s \in \{1,\dots,S\}$ and held-out cell $i$, the draw-specific parameters and the held-out cell's fold-preprocessed features determine its conditional prospectivity probability:

\begin{equation}
\eta_i^{(s)} = \alpha_{d(i)}^{(s)} + \dots, \qquad p_i^{(s)} = \mathrm{logit}^{-1}(\eta_i^{(s)}) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-\eta_i^{(s)})}.
\end{equation}

The reported out-of-fold (OOF) prediction is the posterior mean conditional probability:

\begin{equation}
\bar{p}_i = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S p_i^{(s)}.
\end{equation}

No classification threshold is applied to generate predictions or compute discrimination metrics. Performance is evaluated across the continuous distribution of predicted probabilities using threshold-free summaries \citep{Lobo2008}. ROC-AUC measures rank discrimination. PR-AUC is reported as non-interpolated average precision, $\mathrm{AP}=\sum_n (R_n-R_{n-1})P_n$ over the distinct decision thresholds returned by the precision-recall algorithm, and therefore gives more weight to the low-prevalence positive class than trapezoidal interpolation of that curve; its no-skill value equals the positive prevalence. Brier score is the mean squared difference between the posterior-mean probability and the binary compiled label.

\subsection{Uncertainty concepts}

Four distinct uncertainty concepts apply to this analysis:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Parameter posterior uncertainty:} Represented by MCMC posterior samples of coefficients and hyperparameters.
\item \textbf{Posterior uncertainty in the conditional probability:} For each held-out cell, the posterior distribution of $p_i$ is induced by parameter uncertainty. Posterior means, medians, standard deviations, and 95\% credible intervals quantify uncertainty in $p_i$, not whether a deposit will occur.
\item \textbf{Future outcome uncertainty:} The model yields conditional probability estimates, not simulated Bernoulli realizations $Y_i^{(s)} \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_i^{(s)})$.
\item \textbf{Performance metric uncertainty:} Fold and pooled metric intervals use paired row-bootstrap resampling of fixed out-of-fold scores. These intervals condition on the fixed predictions and do not include model-refitting uncertainty or magnitude of cross-fold score-scale differences, and their tail probabilities are resampling summaries rather than posterior probabilities of geological truth.
\end{enumerate}

Throughout, fitted coefficients and predictor-component shares describe posterior statistical associations, not causal geological effects.

\subsection{Decomposition of the out-of-fold linear predictor}

To describe how additive predictor components vary across held-out cells in each sector, the out-of-fold linear predictor is decomposed for each test cell $i$ in fold $k$:

\begin{equation}
\eta_i = c_{\mathrm{intercept},i} + c_{\mathrm{fault},i} + c_{\mathrm{lith},i} + c_{\mathrm{grav},i} + c_{\mathrm{rocks},i},
\end{equation}

where each component represents the posterior expectation across retained MCMC draws:
\begin{align}
c_{\mathrm{fault},i} &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{post}}\!\left[\beta_{f,\mathrm{lin},d(i)} z_{f,i} + \beta_{f,\mathrm{sq},d(i)} z_{f,i}^2\right],\\
c_{\mathrm{lith},i} &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{post}}\!\left[\beta_{l,\mathrm{lin},d(i)} z_{l,i} + \beta_{l,\mathrm{sq},d(i)} z_{l,i}^2\right],\\
c_{\mathrm{grav},i} &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{post}}\!\left[\beta_g z_{g,i}\right],\\
c_{\mathrm{rocks},i} &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{post}}\!\left[\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{rock},i}^{\mathsf T} \boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{rock}}\right].
\end{align}

For each covariate component $m \in \{\mathrm{fault}, \mathrm{lith}, \mathrm{grav}, \mathrm{rocks}\}$, its dispersion across the $N_{\mathrm{test}}$ held-out cells of fold $k$ is measured by its standard deviation:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SD}_m = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{test}}} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}^{(k)}} (c_{m,i} - \bar{c}_m)^2}.
\end{equation}

The reported component share is the SD-normalized share of additive-component dispersion:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Share}_m = \frac{\mathrm{SD}_m}{\mathrm{SD}_{\mathrm{fault}} + \mathrm{SD}_{\mathrm{lith}} + \mathrm{SD}_{\mathrm{grav}} + \mathrm{SD}_{\mathrm{rocks}}} \times 100\%.
\end{equation}

The intercept component is excluded. It is constant within a domain, so its dispersion across held-out cells measures only the domain composition of the fold and the spread of the domain intercepts, not how predictor values vary among cells; in Folds~3 and~4, which are almost entirely NRB\_3a, it would be close to zero by construction. Excluding it makes the shares describe the covariate components only. They measure relative dispersion of additive components across held-out cells and are not proportions of variance explained.

\subsection{Reference model}
\label{sec:comparator}

The reference model is a non-hierarchical global logistic regression fitted separately within each training fold under the same four-fold design. It uses exactly the same four predictors and the same distance terms as the DVHM---fault distance, lithological-contact distance, Bouguer gravity, and surficial lithology---but replaces the domain-varying coefficients with single global coefficients. This predictor-matched specification holds predictor choice constant and provides a reference for the pooled discrimination of the domain-varying formulation.

\paragraph{Stationary-point diagnostic.} For a quadratic specification the fitted logit surface as a function of distance $x$ has a stationary point at
\begin{equation}
D^* = -\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{lin}}}{2\beta_{\mathrm{sq}}},
\end{equation}
in the units of the predictor used for fitting. Model A uses standardized raw distance, so $D^*_{\mathrm{km}} = \sigma_x D^* + \mu_x$; Model B uses standardized $\log(1+x_{\mathrm{km}})$, so $D^*_{\mathrm{km}} = \exp(\sigma_x D^* + \mu_x) - 1$. Here $\mu_x$ and $\sigma_x$ are the training-fold mean and standard deviation of the transformed distance, so $D^*$ is computed per fold. The linear Models C and D have no stationary point. Where $\beta_{\mathrm{sq}}>0$ the stationary point is a minimum of the logit surface and where $\beta_{\mathrm{sq}}<0$ it is a maximum. $D^*$ is therefore a property of the fitted functional form, not a measured distance to mineralization.

Across the four spatial folds, six Daly domains, and two distance predictors (fault and lithological contact), there are $4 \times 6 \times 2 = 48$ comparison cases. In each case, Model A (raw-quadratic) is compared against Model B (log-quadratic) using three criteria specified before the comparison. Thirty-two of the 48 cases lie in the four domains without positive cells, where domain curvature is informed mainly by partial pooling; results are therefore reported for all 48 cases and separately for the 16 informative cases in NRB\_3a and NRB\_3b. The thresholds are screening values rather than statistical tests: 0.15 allows a moderate difference in the posterior probability of the curvature sign, and 25\% allows a stationary point to move by a quarter of its distance before the two representations are said to disagree.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Criterion 1 (curvature agreement):} The absolute difference in posterior probability of positive quadratic curvature between representations must be less than $0.15$:
\begin{equation}
\left| P(\beta_{\mathrm{sq}} > 0 \mid \text{Model A}) - P(\beta_{\mathrm{sq}} > 0 \mid \text{Model B}) \right| < 0.15.
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Criterion 2 ($D^*$ agreement):} The relative difference between posterior median stationary points $D^*_A$ and $D^*_B$ (in kilometres) must not exceed 25\%:
\begin{equation}
\frac{|D^*_A - D^*_B|}{\max(|D^*_A|, |D^*_B|)} \le 0.25.
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Criterion 3 (training support):} Both median stationary points must lie within the range of the corresponding training distances:
\begin{equation}
s^{\min}_A \le D^*_A \le s^{\max}_A \quad \text{and} \quad s^{\min}_B \le D^*_B \le s^{\max}_B.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

A relationship is classified as representationally robust if and only if it satisfies all three criteria simultaneously.

Because these intervals include negative distances and values far exceeding the width of the belt, the data do not usefully constrain the population-level $D^*$.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Predictor associations and conditional inference}

The out-of-fold results indicate that fault distance, contact distance, Bouguer gravity, and surficial lithology together carry predictive signal for compiled occurrence labels in the modelling frame, although that signal lies in the ranking of cells rather than in calibrated probability levels (Section~\ref{sec:res-transfer}). Because coefficients are conditional on the other predictors and partially pooled across domains, they describe associations rather than separable geological effects. Contact-distance coefficients in particular refer to proximity to any internal contact in a surficial-lithology map (Section~\ref{sec:data}), not to a specific stratigraphic horizon. The two domains that contain positive cells differ in fitted form: NRB\_3a shows a consistent convex contact-distance response and a negative fault-distance association, whereas in NRB\_3b the contact-distance association is negative but the direction of the fault-distance association is not resolved (Section~\ref{sec:res-coefficients}). With 31 positive cells in NRB\_3b, the absence of a resolved fault association may reflect limited data rather than a different relationship. The surficial-lithology terms are associations with the class attribute of the nearest contact, not with the lithology of the cell (Section~\ref{sec:data}), and carry no host-rock interpretation.

\subsection{Spatial non-stationarity and geographic transferability}

Predictor-component shares differ among the four sectors: fault distance dominates in the south-eastern sector, whereas the Bouguer gravity share increases toward the north-west. The regional literature documents along-belt variation in basin structure and stratigraphic setting \citep{Cailteux2005, Selley2005, Hitzman2012, Ndonfack2025}, which could produce such differences. Two statistical factors must be weighed alongside a geological explanation. First, component shares depend on the spread of predictor values within each sector as well as on the fitted coefficients, so a sector with a wide range of fault distances can show a large fault share even under a uniform relationship. Second, the sectors differ in domain composition---Fold~1 consists of CRZ\_4b, NKB, and NRB\_3b cells, whereas Folds~3 and~4 are almost entirely NRB\_3a---so share differences partly reflect the domain-varying coefficients.

Spatially separated validation shows that predictive skill varies by sector. Relative to the reference model, all fold-level difference intervals include zero. The reference model differs from the DVHM in both domain intercepts and domain slopes, so the pooled difference cannot be attributed to slope heterogeneity alone. The domain-stratified contrast is small in NRB\_3a but reverses in NRB\_3b, where the DVHM reaches an ROC-AUC of $0.667$ and the reference model $0.718$. NRB\_3b contributes only 31 positive cells, 22 of them in Fold~2, so this difference is descriptive rather than a stable ranking of the specifications. Domain-stratified results are therefore secondary descriptive summaries; fold transfer remains the primary analysis. A pooled metric alone would conceal that heterogeneity \citep{Roberts2017, Valavi2019}. Daly's tectonic domains \citep{Daly2025} provide a geologically motivated grouping for partial pooling, but the analysis estimates empirical associations within the compiled data and does not test Daly's tectonic interpretation.

\subsection{Representation sensitivity}

Where the data resolve their direction, proximity associations persist across mathematical transformations, but polynomial curvature and the derived stationary point $D^*$ are sensitive to functional form. The near-zero pass rate (1 of 48 cases) is consistent with $D^*$ being a representation-dependent property of the fitted quadratic rather than an invariant physical quantity. Two mechanisms can produce this pattern and the criteria do not separate them: posterior imprecision, since the population $D^*$ intervals span several hundred kilometres, and a genuine shift in fitted curvature between representations. Either way, quadratic extrema should not be translated into drilling targets.

Under raw distance the quadratic term gives a higher fold-mean ROC-AUC than the linear term (Model A $0.725$ vs Model C $0.687$), whereas under log distance the quadratic term is slightly lower (Model B $0.707$ vs Model D $0.722$). These contrasts are not complexity-matched, because the quadratic specifications contain one additional distance term per predictor; they describe the evaluated specifications rather than establish a preferred functional form. Within the matched representation pairs, A versus B and C versus D, the differences are small relative to the fold-to-fold spread of 0.603--0.875. Mean average precision ranges from 0.126 for Model A to 0.200 for Model D and rests on the same four fold values.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several methodological limitations apply:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Sample size and domain imbalance:} Only 138 cells carry a compiled occurrence label, with 107 in NRB\_3a and 31 in NRB\_3b. Four domains contain no positive labels, so within-domain discrimination cannot be estimated there, and two of them are absent from one training partition each. The absence of positive labels does not establish that those domains are unmineralized; it means that their domain-level relationships cannot be evaluated from the observed labels.
\item \textbf{Occurrence labels:} Background cells denote the absence of a compiled record, not verified absence, so the labels reflect where exploration has reported occurrences as well as where mineralization exists. This affects the interpretation of predicted probabilities, apparent false positives, and transfer to poorly explored areas. The source records have 1,000\,m horizontal positional accuracy, one-fifth of the 5\,km cell size, so records near cell edges can shift between adjacent cells. Fitted probabilities describe the conditional probability of a compiled cell label under the model and validation design, not the probability of discovery.
\item \textbf{Residual spatial dependence and validation partitioning:} Contiguous spatial blocking reduces optimistic bias from nearby observations, but it does not guarantee independence between training and validation cells, and spatial autocorrelation may persist across block boundaries \citep{Roberts2017}. The four discrete folds approximate the continuous along-belt geographic and metallogenic gradient and cannot capture finer-scale continuity or heterogeneity; the resulting validation estimates are conditional on the selected partition.
\item \textbf{Input-layer uncertainty:} Predictor uncertainty is not propagated through the model, so posterior intervals are conditional on the prepared input layers and do not capture uncertainty from measurement, georeferencing, interpolation, or cartographic representation. Posterior uncertainty is therefore not equivalent to total geological uncertainty: it does not include uncertainty in the source datasets, occurrence-label completeness, or spatial representation. Although the same input layers are used across model specifications, shared predictor uncertainty may still affect coefficient estimates and contrasts among specifications.
\item \textbf{Predictor reconstruction:} The fault-distance predictor derives from a digitized structural compilation whose underlying geometry is not fully preserved (Section~\ref{sec:data}); the gravity product identity is inferred from companion grids; and domain labels were digitized from a figure raster, with 49 complete-case background cells differing from a strict centroid-in-polygon join. No positive cell is affected by that domain-assignment discrepancy, but background cells contribute to domain-specific estimates, so implications for domain-level inference cannot be excluded. These reconstruction choices introduce uncertainty into predictor values, domain allocation, and the spatial representation used in all model fits. The surficial-lithology class is an attribute of the nearest contact rather than the lithology of the cell, with an undocumented choice between the classes on either side of that contact in almost every cell (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
\item \textbf{Two-dimensional geological proxies:} The distance predictors measure proximity to features mapped in two-dimensional regional datasets. In particular, contact distance is the centroid-to-contact distance defined in Section~\ref{sec:data}, measured in a surficial-lithology map, not distance to a subsurface contact or mineralized horizon. The predictors do not explicitly represent fault dip, subsurface stratigraphy, depth to basement, or the three-dimensional geometry of mineralized systems. Their coefficients should be interpreted as associations with mapped surface and regional geological features, not as direct measurements of subsurface geological relationships or causal distances to mineralization.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}

This study applied a domain-varying hierarchical Bayesian model (DVHM) to mineral prospectivity mapping across the Central African Copperbelt, examining how predictor--occurrence relationships vary across tectonic domains and how those relationships perform under spatially separated validation. Rather than imposing a single set of predictor relationships across the entire belt, the model allows their contributions to vary across domains, providing a framework for representing geological heterogeneity within a regional prospectivity assessment.

The fitted model reveals spatial variation in predictor contributions along the belt. Fault distance has a stronger relative contribution in the south-eastern sector, while the contribution of Bouguer gravity increases toward the north-west. These differences are consistent with variation in the geological context of the evaluated sectors, although they also reflect differences in predictor distributions and domain composition. The results illustrate why regional prospectivity modelling may benefit from allowing predictor relationships to vary spatially rather than assuming that a common relationship applies throughout the study area.

Under four-fold spatially separated validation, the DVHM achieves a pooled out-of-fold ROC-AUC of 0.716. As a reference, the matched global logistic model achieves 0.674 using the same four predictors. The comparison provides additional context for the predictive performance of the heterogeneous formulation, while the fold-level results show that discrimination varies across the evaluated sectors, with DVHM ROC-AUC ranging from 0.875 in the south-east to 0.603 in the north-west. The model therefore demonstrates useful regional discrimination, but its predictive performance is spatially uneven. Its principal predictive capability lies in ranking cells rather than producing probability estimates substantially differentiated from the overall occurrence prevalence: the Brier skill score against a training-prevalence baseline is only $+0.01$, and the calibration slope of 0.61 shows that predicted probabilities are too extreme.

Where the data resolve their direction, negative distance associations recur across the evaluated model representations, whereas quadratic curvature and the stationary point $D^*$ are sensitive to predictor representation. The available data do not adequately constrain a population-level $D^*$, and quadratic extrema should consequently not be interpreted as operational exploration distances. Posterior credible intervals for cell-level conditional probabilities are narrow across most of the study frame, but these estimates remain conditional on the specified model and prepared input layers.

Overall, the study demonstrates the application of hierarchical Bayesian prospectivity modelling to a region characterised by substantial geological and spatial heterogeneity. It shows how domain-varying predictor relationships can be incorporated into regional prospectivity assessment and how their fitted contributions can be examined alongside spatially separated predictive performance. The results provide a basis for further investigation of spatially varying geological associations in mineral prospectivity modelling, while recognising that validation across the selected sectors does not guarantee transferability to frontier areas or geological settings outside the evaluated frame.

\section{Reproducibility, data, and code availability}

The analyses use Python 3.10.11 with PyMC 5.25.1 \citep{Salvatier2016}, PyTensor 2.31.7, ArviZ 0.23.4 \citep{Kumar2019}, scikit-learn 1.7.2 \citep{Pedregosa2011}, NumPy 2.2.6, pandas 2.3.3, SciPy 1.15.3, GeoPandas 1.1.3, Shapely 2.1.2, and rasterio 1.4.4 (GDAL 3.10.3); maps were composed in QGIS 3.40. Because the QGIS Python runtime could not load the compiled scikit-learn extension, standardization and ROC-AUC in the fold fits used numerically equivalent in-repository implementations; the model specification was unchanged. Code, the modelling table, the posterior samples of all four fold fits, the out-of-fold predictions, and the derived results are provided at \url{https://github.com/vmtasi/copperbelt-mineral-prospectivity}, with SHA-256 checksums for every benchmark file in \texttt{paper\_1/benchmark/}. The posterior samples of the distance-representation sensitivity models are archived separately (DOI to be added). The USGS occurrence compilation, the Bouguer gravity grid, and the Africa surficial lithology raster are cited above but are not redistributed with the repository. The modelling frame is reproducible as the intersection of the 5\,km grid with the five USGS permissive tracts. The reconstruction of each predictor, including preparation steps that were not recorded at the time, and the verification of the fitted posterior samples against the modelling table are documented in the Supplementary Material.
