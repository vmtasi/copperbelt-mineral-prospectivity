\title[Rankings Transfer but Base Rates Do Not]{Rankings Transfer but Base Rates Do Not: Leave-One-Sector-Out Evaluation of a Prospectivity Model along the Central African Copperbelt}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Van Mutundo} \sur{Tasi}\,\orcid{https://orcid.org/0009-0001-8512-6950}}\email{vanmutundo@gmail.com}

\affil*[1]{\orgname{Independent researcher}, \orgaddress{\country{Kenya}}}

\abstract{Mineral prospectivity models are usually validated where they were trained but are used to rank ground elsewhere. We evaluated how a Bayesian prospectivity model transfers between four contiguous sectors of the Central African Copperbelt, and why it fails. The model relates the presence of a compiled occurrence record in 1,872 nominal 5\,km cells (138 positive) to distance to faults, distance to lithological contacts and Bouguer gravity, with a complementary log-log link and cell-area offset. All analyses were pre-specified, and all models were fitted by Markov chain Monte Carlo. Trained on three sectors, the model ranked the cells of the fourth with a pooled ROC-AUC of 0.721 (95\% interval 0.687--0.755) and sector values of 0.727--0.834, against 0.781 under random cross-validation. Probabilities did not transfer: the south-eastern, Zambian sector was over-predicted (mean predicted probability 0.130 against an observed rate of 0.019) and the north-western sector under-predicted (0.081 against 0.130), under every model variant and partition. Oracle decompositions attribute most of the south-eastern loss to a base-rate shift that an intercept correction would remove (+0.098 log-score units per cell). Rescaling the slopes added little, and a locally refitted model helped only in the sector that straddles the Zambia--Democratic Republic of the Congo border. Contact distance transferred, Bouguer gravity was informative only in the south-east, and the fault-distance association depended on uneven map coverage; these predictor-level conclusions changed with the sector boundaries. Transferred models should be used for ranking, recalibrated before their probabilities are compared between areas, and validated on held-out regions reported separately.}

\keywords{Mineral prospectivity mapping, Spatial transferability, Spatial cross-validation, Probability calibration, Area of applicability, Central African Copperbelt}

\section{Introduction}

Regional mineral prospectivity mapping combines geological, geochemical and geophysical evidence to rank ground by its association with known mineralization. Approaches range from knowledge-driven evidence integration \citep{BonhamCarter1994, Carranza2009} to weights of evidence \citep{BonhamCarter1989}, logistic regression and machine-learning classifiers \citep{Porwal2006, Carranza2015, Ford2019, Yousefi2021, Lawley2022}. Whatever the method, a data-driven model is trained where occurrences are known and is then used to rank ground where they are not. Its value therefore depends on transfer: whether relationships learned in explored districts still rank cells correctly, and still give meaningful probabilities, in districts the model has not seen.

Standard validation does not measure this. Random cross-validation places neighbouring cells, which share covariates and occurrence histories, on both sides of the split. It therefore estimates interpolation within the sampled region rather than prediction into a new one \citep{Brenning2012, Roberts2017}. Spatial blocking reduces that optimism \citep{Valavi2019, Ploton2020}, and \citet{Airola2019} combined spatial and leave-pair-out cross-validation to obtain reliable ROC-AUC estimates for prospectivity classifiers. Random cross-validation remains appropriate for map accuracy within a probability-sampled region \citep{Wadoux2021}, so the right design depends on the prediction target. For a model meant to rank unexplored districts, that target is a region held out as a whole. Transfer learning between mineral belts has also been proposed as a way to improve prediction where training data are scarce \citep{Lauzon2024}.

Three questions remain largely unanswered in prospectivity studies. First, does a model that ranks well in a held-out region also give calibrated probabilities there? Discrimination and calibration are distinct properties \citep{VanCalster2019}, but prospectivity models are usually summarized by ROC-AUC alone. Second, when transfer fails, is it because the overall frequency of occurrences differs between regions, because the strength of the relationships differs, or because the relationships themselves differ? Each cause implies a different remedy. Third, which individual predictors carry over, and are such conclusions stable when the regions are drawn differently?

We address these questions in the Central African Copperbelt, where basin architecture, stratigraphy and structural style vary along the belt \citep{Cailteux2005, Selley2005, Hitzman2012, Ndonfack2025}. We divide the belt into four contiguous sectors and evaluate a deliberately simple Bayesian model, trained on three sectors and tested on the fourth. The model uses three regional predictors and treats cell area as spatial support. We ask:
\begin{enumerate}
\item How well does the model rank cells in a held-out sector, and are its probabilities calibrated there (Section~\ref{sec:res-loso})?
\item How does transfer depend on the training sectors and on their distance from the target (Section~\ref{sec:res-matrix})?
\item How much of the loss in held-out log score is due to a shifted base rate, to mis-scaled relationships, and to different relationships (Section~\ref{sec:res-decomposition})?
\item How far do held-out sectors lie outside the predictor space of the training data, and which predictor relationships transfer (Sections~\ref{sec:res-shift} and~\ref{sec:res-predictors})?
\item How optimistic is random cross-validation, and do the conclusions depend on the model variant or on where the sector boundaries fall (Sections~\ref{sec:res-optimism} and~\ref{sec:res-robustness})?
\end{enumerate}
All analyses, decision rules and the primary partition were fixed in a written specification before any model was fitted.

\section{Study area and data}

\subsection{Modelling frame}
\label{sec:study_area}

The study region is the Central African Copperbelt across the border between northern Zambia and the southern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC; Figure~\ref{fig:study_area}). The spatial frame is a nominal $5\,\mathrm{km}\times5\,\mathrm{km}$ grid in WGS 84 / UTM zone 35S (EPSG:32735). The grid was intersected with the union of the five USGS permissive tracts for sediment-hosted stratabound copper \citep{USGS2014CACB}, which retained 1,899 cells and clipped those at the tract edges. The tracts define the assessment frame; they are neither a geological contact nor the limit of mineralization. Twenty-seven cells without a tectonic-domain label in the prepared table, one of them positive, were excluded in earlier work on these data. We keep the resulting frame of 1,872 cells so that all results refer to the same cells. The frame covers about $21{,}600\,\mathrm{km}^2$ and extends about 560\,km along the axis of the belt. Clipping leaves cells between less than 1 and $25\,\mathrm{km}^2$ in area.

\subsection{Response}
\label{sec:response}

The response is the presence of a compiled occurrence record in a cell, not a count of occurrences. A cell is positive when at least one record of the USGS Central African Copperbelt assessment database falls within it. The database holds 187 point records: 102 prospects, 65 deposits and 20 grouped records, which aggregate sites within 500\,m. In the 1,872-cell frame, 138 cells are positive and 1,734 are background. A background cell contains no compiled record; this is not verified absence of mineralization. The records have a stated horizontal positional accuracy of 1,000\,m, one-fifth of the cell size, so records near cell edges could fall in a neighbouring cell. Predicted probabilities therefore refer to the presence of a compiled record under the model, not to the probability of discovery.

\paragraph{Distance to mapped faults.}
The fault predictor is the distance from each cell centroid to the nearest line of a 126-line digitization of the regional structural compilation in Figure~1b of \citet{Selley2005}. That figure is drawn at a regional scale, and its positional accuracy and georeferencing control are not documented. The stored distances that enter the model were computed before six lines (features 120--125, in the Zambian Copperbelt) were added to the layer. These lines cannot be verified in the source figure. Recomputing the distance from the surviving layer reproduces the stored value within 1\,m in 1,645 cells and differs in 227, including 26 positive cells. The specification fixed the stored distances as primary. A model with the recomputed distances is reported as a sensitivity analysis. A test for leakage of occurrence information into the added lines, recorded with the specification, was negative (Supplementary Section~S1). The compilation is denser in the DRC than in the Zambian part of the frame. We return to this coverage difference in Section~\ref{sec:disc-predictors}.

\paragraph{Distance to lithological contacts.}
The lithology source is the Africa surficial lithology raster \citep{SayreKabuchanga2015}. It compiles twelve geology, soil and lithology databases and mixes bedrock with unconsolidated parent materials, so it is not a bedrock host-rock map. Six classes occur in the frame. Polygonizing the clipped raster and removing the outer tract boundary gives 259 internal contact lines. The predictor is the distance from each cell centroid to the nearest of these lines (minimum 11\,m, median 12.0\,km). The prepared table also contains a categorical lithology attribute. It is the class of a nearby contact line rather than the lithology of the cell, and in earlier tests it did not improve held-out prediction, so it is not used (Supplementary Section~S1).

\paragraph{Bouguer gravity.}
The gravity predictor is the 2 arc-minute WGM2012 complete Bouguer anomaly \citep{BGIWGM2012}, sampled at the grid node nearest each cell centroid without interpolation. The grid's own header names neither the product nor the datum; the product is identified from companion grids in the same distribution (Supplementary Section~S1).

\subsection{Sectors}
\label{sec:sectors}

Cell centroids were projected onto their first principal component, which follows the north-west--south-east trend of the belt. Positions were measured in kilometres from the south-eastern end, and the ordered cells were divided into four contiguous sectors of 468 cells each (Table~\ref{tab:sectors}; Figure~\ref{fig:partitions}a). Sector~1 lies at the south-eastern end, almost entirely in Zambia. Sector~2 straddles the Zambia--DRC border, and Sectors~3 and~4 lie in the DRC. The proportion of positive cells rises from 1.9\% in Sector~1 to 13.0\% in Sector~4; overall, 31 of the 712 cells in Zambia (4.4\%) and 107 of the 1,160 cells in the DRC (9.2\%) are positive. Geography and geology are therefore confounded: the sectors differ in country, structural setting and occurrence density at the same time, and the design cannot separate these factors.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Transfer model}
\label{sec:model}

If compiled occurrences arise in cell $i$ with intensity $\lambda_i$ per km$^2$, the number of records is $N_i\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_i A_i)$ for clipped area $A_i$, and the presence of at least one record has probability
\begin{equation}
P(Y_i=1)=1-\exp(-\lambda_i A_i),\qquad \mathrm{cloglog}\,P(Y_i=1)=\log\lambda_i+\log A_i .
\end{equation}
The model therefore uses a complementary log-log link with $\log A_i$ as a fixed offset \citep{Baddeley2010, Warton2010}. The log intensity is
\begin{equation}
\log\lambda_i=\alpha+\beta_1 z_{f,i}+\beta_2 z_{f,i}^2+\beta_3 z_{c,i}+\beta_4 z_{c,i}^2+\beta_5 z_{g,i},
\end{equation}
where $z_f$, $z_c$ and $z_g$ are fault distance, contact distance and Bouguer gravity, standardized with the mean and standard deviation of the training cells only; the squared terms are formed after standardization. All coefficients have Normal(0,\,1) priors, and the intercept prior is Normal with mean equal to the logarithm of the training occurrence rate per km$^2$ and standard deviation 1. The model has no domain, sector or spatial random effects, so everything it learns about a held-out sector comes from the predictor values of that sector. Area describes spatial support only; it is not a correction for exploration effort.

\subsection{Estimation and specification}
\label{sec:estimation}

All models were fitted in PyMC \citep{Salvatier2016} with the No-U-Turn sampler \citep{Hoffman2014}. The four primary leave-one-sector-out fits used four chains of 1,200 draws after 1,800 tuning steps (target acceptance 0.97). The 102 further fits used two chains of 1,000 draws after 1,500 tuning steps (target acceptance 0.97), with a recorded seed for each fit. A fit was accepted only with no divergent transitions, rank-normalized $\hat R<1.05$, and bulk and tail effective sample sizes of at least 200 for every parameter. All 106 fits met these criteria at the first attempt: across the primary fits the maximum $\hat R$ was 1.002 and the minimum bulk effective sample size 1,643; across the further fits these were 1.009 and 284. Held-out predictions are posterior means of the cell probability over all draws.

The specification, including the model, the metrics, every analysis below, the decision rules and the primary partition, was written and its SHA-256 hash recorded before any model was fitted. It allowed maximum a posteriori estimation for the secondary analyses if its pooled held-out log score was within 0.002 per cell of full MCMC in the primary fits. It differed by $-0.0046$ per cell, mostly in Sector~1, where the plug-in estimate was overconfident. A versioned amendment, recorded before any secondary fit, therefore replaced it with full MCMC everywhere and, to keep the computation feasible, reduced the random cross-validation from 20 to 10 repeats. Nothing else was changed (Supplementary Section~S2).

\subsection{Validation designs}
\label{sec:designs}

\paragraph{Leave-one-sector-out (primary).} Each sector is held out in turn, the model is fitted to the other three, and the held-out sector is predicted. Pooled metrics combine the four sets of held-out predictions; sector-level metrics are reported alongside, because pooling mixes four separately fitted models.

\paragraph{Sector-to-sector transfer.} To see how transfer depends on the source, the model was also trained on each single sector and on each pair of sectors, and tested on every sector not used for training. Single-sector fits trained on Sector~1, which has nine positive cells, are flagged and not interpreted on their own; the specification set this flag at fewer than 15 training positives. Sector separation is the distance between sector centroids along the belt axis.

\paragraph{Random cross-validation.} For comparison, the model was fitted under ten repeats of four-fold cross-validation with folds drawn at random, stratified by the response.

\subsection{Scores}
\label{sec:scores}

The primary score is the mean held-out log score, $y_i\log\bar p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-\bar p_i)$, a strictly proper scoring rule that rewards both ranking and probability accuracy \citep{Gneiting2007}; higher is better. Because the prevalence differs between sectors, raw log scores cannot be compared across sectors. We therefore report log-score skill: the model's score minus that of a constant prediction equal to the smoothed prevalence of the training cells, $(k+0.5)/(n+1)$ for $k$ positives among $n$ training cells. Positive skill means the model is better than knowing only the base rate of the training data.

Secondary scores are ROC-AUC, average precision (non-interpolated; its no-skill value equals the prevalence) and Brier skill against the same reference. Calibration is summarized by two quantities \citep{Cox1958, VanCalster2019}. Calibration-in-the-large is the intercept $a$ in $\mathrm{logit}\,P(Y=1)=a+\mathrm{logit}(\bar p)$ with the slope fixed at 1; it is zero when the overall level is right, and negative when predictions are too high. The calibration slope comes from the joint fit of intercept and slope; a slope below 1 means predictions are too spread out. We also compare the mean predicted probability with the observed rate in each sector.

ROC-AUC intervals are 95\% class-stratified bootstrap intervals (2,000 resamples). Intervals for mean log scores and for differences in log score use a spatial-block bootstrap that resamples $25\,\mathrm{km}\times25\,\mathrm{km}$ blocks (2,000 replicates), so that neighbouring cells are resampled together. Both condition on the fixed held-out predictions and do not include refitting uncertainty.

\subsection{Decomposing the transfer loss}
\label{sec:decomposition}

For each held-out sector $k$ we computed four log scores on the same cells. The last three use the labels of sector $k$ and are diagnostic oracles, not models that could be deployed:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $L_{\mathrm{transfer}}$: the leave-one-sector-out prediction.
\item $L_{\mathrm{base}}$: the transfer prediction with its intercept re-estimated on sector $k$, i.e.\ $\mathrm{cloglog}\,p_i=\eta_i+\delta$ with $\delta\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,1)$, where $\eta_i$ is the cloglog of the transfer prediction. This corrects the base rate only.
\item $L_{\mathrm{recal}}$: as $L_{\mathrm{base}}$, with a single multiplier $m\sim\mathrm{Normal}(1,1)$ on the transfer linear predictor excluding the area offset. This corrects the overall strength of the relationships as well.
\item $L_{\mathrm{local}}$: the same model fitted within sector $k$ by four-fold inner cross-validation, with folds formed from intact $25\,\mathrm{km}$ blocks balanced for cell and positive counts, so that no block contributes to both training and testing.
\end{enumerate}
The gaps $L_{\mathrm{base}}-L_{\mathrm{transfer}}$, $L_{\mathrm{recal}}-L_{\mathrm{base}}$ and $L_{\mathrm{local}}-L_{\mathrm{recal}}$ measure the loss attributable to the base-rate shift, to mis-scaled relationships, and to relationships that differ in form. The oracle corrections were estimated by MCMC and evaluated on the cells used to estimate them; with one or two parameters per sector, the resulting optimism is small. The local fits use about three-quarters of one sector each, so the last gap also reflects the smaller training set. In Sector~1 the inner training folds hold 3--8 positive cells, and that gap is not interpreted.

\subsection{Covariate shift}
\label{sec:shift-methods}

For each held-out sector we computed the share of cells outside the 1st--99th percentile of the training values of each model term. We also computed the dissimilarity index and the area of applicability of \citet{Meyer2021} on the five standardized model terms with equal weights. A held-out cell lies inside the area of applicability when its distance to the nearest training cell in predictor space, divided by the mean nearest-neighbour distance among training cells, does not exceed a threshold derived from the training data alone: the largest cross-validated training dissimilarity (five folds) after removing values above the upper Tukey fence.

\subsection{Which predictors transfer}
\label{sec:predictor-rule}

The rule was fixed in the specification. A predictor transfers when two conditions hold: every coefficient of that predictor (linear and, where present, quadratic) keeps the same sign in at least three of the four leave-one-sector-out fits; and the oriented within-sector ROC-AUC of the predictor alone has a 95\% interval above 0.5 in at least three of the four sectors. It partly transfers when exactly one condition holds, and does not transfer otherwise. Orientation scores closer distances and lower gravity as more prospective. We also report the coefficients from the single-sector fits.

\subsection{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness-methods}

Three model variants were specified as sensitivity analyses: without the squared terms; with the recomputed fault distances; and with a fixed indicator for the 195 cells outside the Northern Rift Basin of \citet{Daly2025}, none of which contains a compiled occurrence. They are reported but were not used for conclusions.

Two alternative partitions use the same principal axis (Figure~\ref{fig:partitions}b,c). The first cuts the axis at the midpoints of the three largest gaps between consecutive positive cells, subject to every sector being at least 100\,km long; this approximates holding out whole mining districts. The second cuts it so that each sector holds 34 or 35 positive cells, which gives every single-sector training set the same support. Its two north-western sectors are only 49 and 71\,km long and their boundaries cross dense clusters of positive cells, which makes it optimistic. Under each partition we repeated the leave-one-sector-out fits and the single-sector transfer matrix. By the pre-specified rule, conclusions are robust to the partition only if the predictor verdicts and the direction of the main transfer results agree under all three partitions.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Transfer to a held-out sector}
\label{sec:res-loso}

Trained on three sectors, the model ranked the cells of the fourth better than chance in every sector (Table~\ref{tab:loso}; Figure~\ref{fig:sector}a). Pooled ROC-AUC was 0.721 (95\% interval 0.687--0.755), and sector values ranged from 0.727 in Sector~2 to 0.834 in Sector~1. Average precision exceeded its no-skill value in every sector, by a factor of 3.3 in Sector~1 and 1.8 in Sector~4.

Probabilities transferred much less well. Pooled log-score skill was $+0.009$ per cell, only slightly better than predicting the training base rate. Skill was negative in Sector~1 ($-0.042$), close to zero in Sector~2 ($+0.002$) and positive in Sectors~3 and~4 ($+0.018$ and $+0.059$). The failure is one of level: the model predicted a mean probability of 0.130 in Sector~1, where 1.9\% of cells are positive, and 0.081 in Sector~4, where 13.0\% are (Figure~\ref{fig:sector}b). Calibration-in-the-large was $-2.33$ in Sector~1 and $+0.61$ in Sector~4. Predictions were also too spread out, with calibration slopes of 0.46--0.75 by sector and 0.47 pooled.

\subsection{Transfer between individual sectors}
\label{sec:res-matrix}

Trained on a single sector, the model still ranked cells of other sectors better than chance in all twelve combinations (ROC-AUC 0.56--0.95; Figure~\ref{fig:matrix}a). Its probabilities, however, beat the source base rate in only three combinations, all between neighbouring sectors: Sector~1 to~2 ($+0.070$ per cell, from nine training positives), Sector~2 to~1 ($+0.042$) and Sector~3 to~4 ($+0.046$; Figure~\ref{fig:matrix}b). Skill fell with distance along the belt, to $-0.42$ and $-0.58$ per cell between the two end sectors. Transfer was not symmetric: the mean absolute difference between the log scores of $A\to B$ and $B\to A$ was 0.162 per cell.

More training data did not always help. For Sector~4, a model trained on Sector~3 alone (log score $-0.356$, ROC-AUC 0.723) did as well as one trained on all three other sectors ($-0.368$, 0.740). For Sector~1, a model trained on Sector~2 alone scored better ($-0.078$) than one trained on Sectors~2--4 ($-0.182$). This is consistent with the higher base rate of the north-western sectors being carried into the prediction. The full matrix, including the six two-sector training sets, is in Supplementary Table~S3.

\subsection{Why transfer fails}
\label{sec:res-decomposition}

The decomposition separates the sectors clearly (Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}; Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition}). In Sector~1, re-estimating only the intercept improved the log score by $+0.098$ per cell (95\% interval $+0.020$ to $+0.189$), whereas rescaling the relationships added nothing ($-0.001$). Most of the south-eastern loss is therefore a base-rate shift. In Sectors~2--4 the base-rate and slope corrections were small, and all their intervals include zero.

A locally fitted model beat the recalibrated transfer model only in Sector~2 ($+0.030$; $+0.000$ to $+0.057$), the sector that straddles the border, and the lower end of that interval is at zero. In Sector~4 the local model was worse ($-0.032$; $-0.056$ to $-0.013$): data from the other sectors predicted Sector~4 better than three-quarters of Sector~4 itself. There is therefore little evidence that the relationships differ in form between sectors, except possibly in Sector~2.

\subsection{Covariate shift}
\label{sec:res-shift}

The held-out sectors differ in how far they extend beyond the predictor space of the training data. In Sector~1, 36\% of cells had fault distances outside the 1st--99th percentile of the training values, reflecting the sparse fault compilation in Zambia. In Sector~2, 21\% of cells were outside the training range of gravity, and in Sector~3, about 20\% were outside that of contact distance. In Sector~4 no term exceeded 5\%. Correspondingly, 64\% of cells in Sectors~1 and~2 lay outside the area of applicability, against 41\% in Sector~3 and 11\% in Sector~4 (Figure~\ref{fig:heldout}b).

The positive cells of the south-east lie mostly in this unfamiliar space: 8 of the 9 positive cells in Sector~1 and 29 of 31 in Sector~2 were outside the area of applicability, against 11 of 37 in Sector~3 and 9 of 61 in Sector~4. Ranking in Sectors~1 and~2 was nevertheless no worse than elsewhere (Table~\ref{tab:loso}). Being outside the area of applicability marks where the training data do not cover the predictor combinations; it did not by itself predict poor ranking here. Because positive cells concentrate outside it in the south-east, mean log scores inside and outside the area are dominated by prevalence and are given only in Supplementary Table~S4.

\subsection{Which predictor relationships transfer}
\label{sec:res-predictors}

In all four leave-one-sector-out fits, occurrence intensity was highest close to contacts and faults and decreased with distance over most of the observed range (negative linear and positive quadratic terms), and it decreased with Bouguer gravity. Every 95\% interval excluded zero (Supplementary Table~S5). The within-sector associations of the individual predictors were less uniform (Table~\ref{tab:predictors}; Figure~\ref{fig:predictors}).

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Contact distance transfers.} Positive cells were closer to contacts than background cells in Sectors~1, 2 and~4. The association weakened towards the north-west (ROC-AUC 0.84 in Sector~1 and 0.60 in Sector~4) and was not resolved in Sector~3.
\item \textbf{Bouguer gravity partly transfers.} Positive cells had lower gravity in Sectors~1 and~2 (ROC-AUC 0.85 and 0.78), but not in Sectors~3 and~4 (0.57 and 0.51). Single-sector fits showed the same split: the gravity coefficient was $-1.27$ and $-1.09$ when trained on Sectors~1 and~2, with intervals excluding zero, and $-0.15$ and $-0.07$ when trained on Sectors~3 and~4.
\item \textbf{Fault distance partly transfers.} Positive cells were closer to mapped faults in Sector~3 (ROC-AUC 0.73), unresolved in Sectors~2 and~4, and farther from them in Sector~1 (0.24). The model trained on Sector~1 alone had a positive linear fault coefficient ($+1.70$; 95\% interval $+0.55$ to $+2.92$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Optimism of random cross-validation}
\label{sec:res-optimism}

Random four-fold cross-validation overstated transfer (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). Across ten repeats it gave a mean log score of $-0.232$ (range of the central 95\% of repeats $-0.233$ to $-0.231$) and ROC-AUC of 0.781 (0.778--0.786), against $-0.263$ and 0.721 for leave-one-sector-out. The optimism of 0.031 per cell in log score and 0.06 in ROC-AUC rose to 0.069--0.073 per cell under the two alternative partitions. The narrow spread across random repeats says nothing about the error of transfer to a new sector.

\subsection{Robustness to model variant and partition}
\label{sec:res-robustness}

The base-rate result was the most stable finding (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). The south-eastern sector was over-predicted under every model variant and every partition, with calibration-in-the-large between $-1.13$ and $-3.26$. Sector~4 was under-predicted under every model variant ($+0.56$ to $+0.80$) and under the gap partition ($+0.73$). Under the equal-positive partition, the under-prediction moved to its short Sector~3 ($+0.78$).

Pooled ROC-AUC varied less, from 0.675 to 0.743 across model variants and from 0.686 to 0.721 across partitions. Ranking in Sector~1, however, depended strongly on specification. Its ROC-AUC was 0.834 for the primary model, 0.876 with the indicator for cells outside the Northern Rift Basin, 0.480 with the recomputed fault distances, and 0.364 without the squared terms. With nine positive cells, the Sector~1 ranking result should not be quoted without this range. The model with recomputed fault distances had a better pooled log score than the primary model ($-0.246$ against $-0.263$). The stored distances remain primary because the specification fixed them, not because of their performance.

The predictor verdicts were not fully robust to the partition. Under the gap partition all three verdicts matched the primary partition. Under the equal-positive partition, contact distance was useful in only one of four sectors and dropped to ``partly transfers'' (Table~\ref{tab:predictors}). By the pre-specified rule, the predictor-level conclusions are therefore not robust to the partition.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Ranking transfers; probability does not}

The model ranked cells in every held-out sector, and pooled ROC-AUC stayed between 0.68 and 0.74 under every model variant and partition. Its probabilities, however, were set by the base rate of the training sectors. They were too high at the south-eastern end and too low at the north-western end, under every specification. A model trained on part of the belt therefore told us where within a new sector to look first, but not how much mineralization to expect there.

The distinction matters in practice. Rankings support decisions about where to look within an area, whereas probabilities feed decisions that compare areas, such as allocating budget between districts or estimating expected numbers of targets. A transferred model can be used for the first without recalibration but not for the second. The decomposition shows that in the most affected sector a single intercept would have recovered most of the loss. Because that correction needs some information about the target, such as a few known occurrences or an independent estimate of its base rate, the transferred probabilities should be read as relative scores until they are recalibrated.

\subsection{Why base rates shift along the belt}

The base-rate step coincides with the national border. In the frame, 4.4\% of cells in Zambia carry a compiled record, against 9.2\% in the DRC, and the sector that straddles the border is the only one in which a local model improved on the transferred relationships. Several explanations are consistent with this pattern, and the data cannot separate them. The geological settings differ along the belt \citep{Cailteux2005, Selley2005, Hitzman2012}. The number of compiled prospects and deposits per unit area may also reflect differences in exploration history and in how occurrences were documented and compiled on the two sides of the border \citep{USGS2014CACB}. Large deposits recorded as a single point occupy one cell however much metal they contain. Because the response is the presence of a compiled record, any of these mechanisms changes the base rate that a model inherits from its training data. The model has no term that could anticipate such a shift from the predictors alone.

\subsection{Which relationships carry over}
\label{sec:disc-predictors}

Proximity to lithological contacts was the most transferable relationship. Its sign was stable, and it separated positive from background cells in three of four sectors under the primary partition, although less strongly towards the north-west. The contact layer is a surficial compilation, so this is an association with mapped surface contacts rather than with a specific stratigraphic horizon.

The gravity association was regional. Low Bouguer gravity marked positive cells in the two south-eastern sectors and carried no information in the two north-western ones, so its pooled coefficient averages two different situations. Gravity is also correlated with fault and contact distance, so the association should not be read as an isolated geophysical effect.

The fault-distance reversal in Sector~1 most likely reflects the fault map rather than geology. The regional compilation is dense in the DRC and sparse in the Zambian part of the frame, where 36\% of cells lie beyond the training range of fault distance. In such a sector, positive cells can lie far from any mapped fault simply because the faults near them are not in the compilation. This also explains why ranking in Sector~1 changed so much with the fault-distance version and with the squared terms. A predictor whose coverage differs between regions transfers poorly even if the underlying geological relationship holds everywhere.

Earlier work on these data also tested whether letting the intercept or the predictor relationships vary between tectonic domains \citep{Daly2025} improves transfer. Domain-varying distance relationships did not improve held-out prediction, as fixed or as hierarchical effects. A domain-varying gravity relationship improved prediction only in a hierarchical model that had been suggested by the data, and not in a fixed-effect version (Supplementary Section~S7). Adding geological domain structure therefore does not repair transfer in this frame. This is consistent with the decomposition, which locates most of the loss in the base rate rather than in the relationships.

\subsection{Implications for validating prospectivity models}

Random cross-validation overstated held-out skill by 0.031 log-score units per cell and 0.06 in ROC-AUC under the primary partition, and by more under the alternative partitions. Its repeats were nearly identical, so their spread conveyed a precision that did not exist for transfer. This agrees with other spatial applications \citep{Roberts2017, Ploton2020}. The right design still depends on the target \citep{Wadoux2021}: for ranking cells within an explored and well-sampled district, random splitting can be adequate; for ranking a new district, a region must be held out as a whole.

We suggest that prospectivity studies intended for use beyond their training area report four things:
\begin{enumerate}
\item transfer to each held-out region separately, not only pooled;
\item a proper score with its skill against the training base rate, alongside ROC-AUC;
\item calibration-in-the-large or the mean predicted probability against the observed rate;
\item the sensitivity of the conclusions to where the held-out regions are drawn.
\end{enumerate}
The oracle decomposition used here costs two small fits per region and shows whether a failure lies in the base rate, the strength of the relationships or their form, which in turn points to the remedy.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Few transfer units.} Four sectors give four transfer estimates, not a sampling distribution. Sector~1 has nine positive cells, so its ranking estimate is fragile (Section~\ref{sec:res-robustness}). The spatial-block intervals condition on the fixed predictions and do not include refitting uncertainty.
\item \textbf{Partition dependence.} The primary sector boundaries are arbitrary with respect to deposit clusters. Predictor-level conclusions changed under one alternative partition, which is itself optimistic because its boundaries cross dense clusters.
\item \textbf{Confounding.} Country, geological setting, fault-map coverage and occurrence density change together along the belt. The design shows that transfer fails and where, but not which of these factors causes the failure.
\item \textbf{Occurrence labels.} Background cells denote the absence of a compiled record, not verified absence. Base rates therefore reflect exploration and compilation as well as mineralization, and positional uncertainty of 1,000\,m can move records between neighbouring cells.
\item \textbf{Predictors.} The three predictors are regional, two-dimensional proxies. The fault compilation is uneven in coverage and its stored distances could not be fully reproduced; the contact layer is surficial; and the gravity product was identified indirectly. Uncertainty in the input layers is not propagated.
\item \textbf{Residual dependence.} Spatial autocorrelation can cross sector boundaries, so even leave-one-sector-out scores may be optimistic for genuinely new ground. Transfer along one belt is not transfer to a different belt or to frontier terrain.
\item \textbf{Oracle corrections.} The decomposition uses the labels of the held-out sector. It diagnoses the source of the loss; it is not a correction available in practice.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}

A simple prospectivity model trained on three sectors of the Central African Copperbelt ranked cells in the fourth moderately well, with a pooled ROC-AUC of 0.721. Its probabilities did not transfer: they followed the base rate of the training sectors and were too high in the south-east and too low in the north-west under every model variant and partition. Oracle decompositions attribute most of the loss in the worst sector to this base-rate shift. Recalibrating the relationships added little, and a locally refitted model helped only in the sector that straddles the Zambia--DRC border. Contact distance was the most transferable predictor, gravity was informative only in the south-east, and the fault-distance association depended on uneven map coverage. Predictor-level conclusions changed with the partition. Random cross-validation overstated held-out skill.

For regional prospectivity work, the results support using transferred models for ranking within new ground, recalibrating them before their probabilities are compared between areas, and validating them on held-out regions, with scores and calibration reported for each region separately.
